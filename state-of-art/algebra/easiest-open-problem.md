# The easiest open problem in this survey

As of 30 September 2026. Not published on the site.

The judges ranked the seven-element lattice L10, from the finite lattice representation problem, first and the Hadamard matrix of order 668 second. A check against current sources on 30 September found that both have been answered in public, each with at least one check by someone other than its author, and neither answer is refereed yet. The details are under [Settled since the ranking](#settled-since-the-ranking). So the pick moves to the next runner-up that is still open: Lieb's permanental dominance conjecture for $5 \times 5$ matrices.

It is easy only in a narrow sense. The conjecture is one inequality for each subgroup $H$ of $S_5$ and each irreducible character of $H$, and there are 78 such pairs. Known results and three short arguments worked out for this survey settle all but 16 of them, and relabelling brings the 16 down to 10 genuinely different cases. Each is an inequality between two polynomials in the entries of a $5 \times 5$ positive semidefinite matrix, so a proof is a certificate that exact arithmetic can check. Unlike L10, nobody knows the answer: the stronger permanent-on-top conjecture fails at exactly this size. The cost is that an inequality over a continuum can resist every certificate you try, and no one has estimated how long the job takes. Two of the three judges ranked this case third; with the first two answered, it is next in line. For a finite computation whose answer is also unknown, look at Kuzmin's $d(5)$ among the runners-up, which is much heavier.

The survey's entry is in [linear algebra, matrices and representation theory](./linear-algebra-and-representation-theory.md).

## The problem

For an $n \times n$ complex matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ the permanent is

$$
\operatorname{per} A = \sum_{\sigma \in S_n} \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,\sigma(i)},
$$

the determinant without the signs. More generally, for a subgroup $H \le S_n$ and a character $\chi$ of $H$, the *generalized matrix function* is

$$
d^H_\chi(A) = \sum_{\sigma \in H} \chi(\sigma) \prod_{i=1}^{n} a_{i,\sigma(i)} .
$$

With $H = S_n$, the sign character gives $\det A$ and the trivial character gives $\operatorname{per} A$. With $H$ trivial it is the product of the diagonal entries. With $H = S_n$ and $\chi$ irreducible it is called an *immanant*.

Let $A$ be positive semidefinite Hermitian. Then $d^H_\chi(A)$ is a real number, and Schur proved in 1918 that $\det A \le d^H_\chi(A)/\chi(1)$: the determinant is the smallest of these functions once each is divided by $\chi(1)$. Lieb conjectured in 1966 ("Proofs of some conjectures on permanents", J. Math. Mech. 16) that the permanent is the largest:

$$
\frac{d^H_\chi(A)}{\chi(1)} \le \operatorname{per} A .
$$

A reducible character is a sum of irreducible ones, so it is enough to prove this for irreducible $\chi$. Both sides equal $a_{11} a_{22} \cdots a_{nn}$ when $A$ is diagonal, so the inequality has no slack on diagonal matrices. For $n = 2$ the only interesting case is the determinant, and $\operatorname{per} A - \det A = 2 \lvert a_{12} \rvert^2 \ge 0$.

**A small example.** Take $n = 3$, $H = C_3$ generated by $\rho = (1\,2\,3)$, and $\chi(\rho) = \omega = e^{2\pi i/3}$, $\chi(\rho^2) = \bar\omega$. Scaling row and column $i$ by $a_{ii}^{-1/2}$ multiplies both sides by the same positive number (and a zero diagonal entry forces a zero row, where both sides vanish), so assume $a_{11} = a_{22} = a_{33} = 1$. Put

$$
z = a_{12}\, a_{23}\, a_{31}, \qquad S = \lvert a_{12} \rvert^2 + \lvert a_{23} \rvert^2 + \lvert a_{13} \rvert^2 .
$$

Since $A$ is Hermitian, $a_{13} a_{21} a_{32} = \bar z$, and the three transpositions contribute $\lvert a_{12} \rvert^2$, $\lvert a_{23} \rvert^2$ and $\lvert a_{13} \rvert^2$. So

$$
d_\chi(A) = 1 + 2 \operatorname{Re}(\omega z), \qquad \operatorname{per} A = 1 + 2 \operatorname{Re} z + S, \qquad \det A = 1 + 2 \operatorname{Re} z - S .
$$

With $1 - \omega = \sqrt3\, e^{-i\pi/6}$ and $z = r e^{i\theta}$,

$$
\operatorname{per} A - d_\chi(A) = S + 2 \operatorname{Re}\big((1 - \omega) z\big) = S + 2\sqrt3\, r \cos(\theta - \pi/6) .
$$

If the cosine is nonnegative we are done. If not, the arithmetic–geometric mean inequality gives $r \le (S/3)^{3/2}$, so $2\sqrt3\, r \le \tfrac23 S^{3/2}$, which is at most $S$ when $S \le 9/4$. And $S > 9/4$ cannot happen with a negative cosine: then $\det A \ge 0$ gives $2r\cos\theta \ge S - 1 > 5/4$, and $r \le 1$ because each $2 \times 2$ principal minor is nonnegative, so $\cos\theta > 5/8 > 1/2$, which puts $\theta - \pi/6$ between $-\pi/2$ and $\pi/6$, where the cosine is positive. So $d_\chi(A) \le \operatorname{per} A$, with equality only when $A$ is diagonal. (A numerical test on 200,000 random positive semidefinite matrices of ranks 1 to 3, run for this survey, found a smallest value of $(\operatorname{per} A - d_\chi(A))/\operatorname{per} A$ of 0.037.)

The example shows what each case costs. The $2 \times 2$ minors alone are not enough; the proof needs $\det A \ge 0$, and it depends on the phase of the product around the 3-cycle. Every pair $(H, \chi)$ has its own polynomial, and each needs its own argument.

## What is still open

Wanless's 2022 survey (arXiv:2202.01867) records the full conjecture as proved only for $n \le 3$. For immanants much more is known: T. H. Pate proved them for every $n \le 13$ (Proc. London Math. Soc. (3) 76 (1998) 307–358). Two unrefereed preprints from 2026 go further. Siwei Zeng (arXiv:2608.21749, 22 August 2026) proves the full conjecture for $n = 4$: all 37 pairs from the 11 conjugacy classes of subgroups of $S_4$. Thirty-five follow from general inequalities between principal minors, moments and block contractions; the two non-real characters of $A_4$ need sum-of-squares certificates on a cone of $3 \times 3$ Gram matrices. The whole theorem is formalized in Lean 4 as `normalized_permanental_dominance_n4`, in the companion repository EndlessLethe/PermanentalDominanceConjecture. Yinjie Li (arXiv:2609.13412, 11 September 2026) settles the three immanants Pate's work left, $(4,4,3,3)$ at $n = 14$ and $(5,4,3,3)$ and $(3^5)$ at $n = 15$, with certificates checked in exact arithmetic and the order-14 step also in Lean.

If both stand, the open cases are the full conjecture at $n = 5$ and immanants at $n = 16$. The survey names both. This note picks $n = 5$ because its matrices are $5 \times 5$ and its list of cases is short and explicit, while for $n = 16$ the sources don't say which partitions would be hard. It is also where the stronger permanent-on-top conjecture breaks: V. S. Shchesnovich found a rank-2 complex $5 \times 5$ counterexample by numerical search (Linear Algebra Appl. 490, 2016). That route to Lieb's conjecture is closed at this size, and it is a reason to take a counterexample seriously.

$S_5$ has 19 conjugacy classes of subgroups, and summing the numbers of irreducible characters gives 78 pairs $(H, \chi)$. The counts come from a brute-force Python program written for this survey; the same program gives 11 classes and 37 pairs for $S_4$, which matches Zeng's count.

## How to attack it

The plan is to shrink the 78 pairs to a short list by hand, search the list for counterexamples, then look for certificates the way Zeng did at $n = 4$.

1. **Reduce.** Three of the four steps below were worked out for this survey. They are not from the literature or from Zeng, and they need checking before anyone relies on them. They use Lieb's own 1966 inequality: if $A = \begin{pmatrix} B & C \\ C^* & D \end{pmatrix}$ is positive semidefinite then $\operatorname{per} A \ge \operatorname{per} B \cdot \operatorname{per} D$.
   - *$H$ fixes a point* (37 pairs, the subgroups of $S_4$). If every $\sigma \in H$ fixes 5, then $d^H_\chi(A) = a_{55}\, d^H_\chi(A')$ with $A'$ the top-left $4 \times 4$ block, and Lieb's inequality gives $\operatorname{per} A \ge a_{55} \operatorname{per} A'$. Zeng's $n = 4$ theorem finishes.
   - *$H$ has orbits of sizes 3 and 2* (15 pairs). For $C_3 \times C_2$ and $S_3 \times S_2$ the characters are products $\chi_1 \chi_2$ and $d$ factors as $d_{\chi_1}(B)\, d_{\chi_2}(D)$ over the two diagonal blocks. Both factors are nonnegative by Schur, each is at most $\chi_i(1)$ times a permanent by the case $n \le 3$, and Lieb's inequality finishes. The third group is $\langle (1\,2\,3), (1\,2)(4\,5) \rangle \cong S_3$, which swaps 4 and 5 exactly when $\sigma$ is odd, so $a_{44}a_{55}$ or $\lvert a_{45} \rvert^2$ appears according to the sign of $\sigma$. That gives
     $$d^H_\chi(A) = \tfrac12 \big( \operatorname{per} D \cdot d_\chi(B) + \det D \cdot d_{\chi \cdot \mathrm{sgn}}(B) \big) \le \tfrac12 (\operatorname{per} D + \det D)\, \chi(1) \operatorname{per} B \le \chi(1) \operatorname{per} B \operatorname{per} D,$$
     where $d_\chi(B)$ and $d_{\chi \cdot \mathrm{sgn}}(B)$ are immanants of $S_3$, and Lieb's inequality finishes again.
   - *$H = S_5$* (7 pairs). These are immanants, covered by Pate.
   - *$H = A_5$ and $\chi$ of degree 1, 4 or 5* (3 pairs). Then $\chi$ is the restriction of an irreducible character $\psi$ of $S_5$ with $\psi \ne \psi \cdot \mathrm{sgn}$. The indicator function of $A_5$ is $\tfrac12(1 + \mathrm{sgn})$, so $d^{A_5}_\chi = \tfrac12 (d_\psi + d_{\psi \cdot \mathrm{sgn}})$, the average of two immanants, each at most $\psi(1) \operatorname{per} A$.

   That leaves 16 pairs, all on transitive groups: the cyclic group $C_5$ (five linear characters), the dihedral group $D_{10}$ (four characters), the Frobenius group $F_{20} = \mathrm{AGL}(1,5)$ (four linear characters and one of degree 4), and the two characters of $A_5$ of degree 3.
2. **Relabel.** If a permutation $g$ normalizes $H$, replacing $A$ by $(a_{g(i)g(j)})$ leaves the permanent alone and turns $d_\chi$ into $d_{\chi'}$ for the conjugated character. Replacing $A$ by its complex conjugate turns $d_\chi$ into $d_{\bar\chi}$. Multiplication by 2 mod 5 permutes the four nontrivial characters of $C_5$ and swaps the two of degree 2 in $D_{10}$; complex conjugation swaps the two characters of order 4 of $F_{20}$; an odd permutation swaps the two of degree 3 in $A_5$. That leaves 10 cases: 2 for $C_5$, 3 for $D_{10}$, 4 for $F_{20}$ and 1 for $A_5$. Each identification was confirmed numerically on random matrices.
3. **Look for a counterexample first.** Maximize $d_\chi(A) / (\chi(1) \operatorname{per} A)$ over positive semidefinite matrices, starting with rank 2, where Shchesnovich found his. A crude version run for this survey (random starts and hill climbing, on all 16 pairs) found nothing above 1, which says little; a serious search would use proper optimization and many more starts.
4. **Look for certificates.** Scale to unit diagonal, as in the example. Each case is then a real polynomial of degree at most 5 in the 20 real coordinates of the 10 off-diagonal entries, to be shown nonnegative on the $5 \times 5$ correlation matrices. Try Zeng's general inequalities first; at $n = 4$ they handled 35 of 37 pairs. For what survives, write $A$ as the Gram matrix of five unit vectors and search for a sum-of-squares certificate by semidefinite programming, then round it to rationals and verify it exactly. The known trouble spot is the rounding: the inequality is tight on every diagonal matrix, so the certificate has no slack there and has to be exact.
5. **Write it up and formalize it.** Zeng's Lean development is a template for the certificates, and the reductions in step 1 are short.

**How a solution would be checked.** A proof is the reductions of step 1, which a referee reads, plus for each of the 10 cases an identity expressing $\operatorname{per} A - d_\chi(A)/\chi(1)$ as a sum of terms that are visibly nonnegative on positive semidefinite matrices, which a computer expands and compares in exact rational arithmetic. A disproof is one $5 \times 5$ matrix, best given with exact entries, since the margin of a numerical counterexample could be rounding error.

## What it would settle, and what it would not

A proof would push the full conjecture from $n = 4$ to $n = 5$, and it would rest on Zeng's $n = 4$ result, which is not yet refereed. It would say nothing about $n = 6$ or about immanants at $n = 16$. A counterexample would be much bigger news: it would disprove a conjecture that has stood since 1966, and at the same size where permanent-on-top fell.

## Settled since the ranking

### Finite lattice representation problem: the lattice L10

[Homological algebra, category theory, K-theory, universal algebra and lattices](./homological-categorical-and-universal-algebra.md). A *congruence* of an algebra $A$ (a set with operations) is an equivalence relation that every operation respects, and the congruences, ordered by inclusion, form a lattice $\operatorname{Con} A$. For a group they correspond to the normal subgroups: the five subgroups of $\mathbb{Z}/2 \times \mathbb{Z}/2$ (the trivial one, three of order 2, the whole group) form the lattice $M_3$. The problem asks whether every finite lattice is $\operatorname{Con} A$ for a finite algebra $A$. Grätzer and Schmidt proved in 1963 that every algebraic lattice is the congruence lattice of some algebra, so the question is about finiteness, and Wikipedia counts it among the oldest open problems in universal algebra. P. P. Pálfy and P. Pudlák (Algebra Universalis 11, 1980) showed that it is equivalent to asking whether every finite lattice is an interval $[H, G] = \lbrace K : H \le K \le G \rbrace$ in the subgroup lattice of a finite group.

One direction is short. Let $G$ act on the left cosets $G/H$, with one unary operation $xH \mapsto gxH$ for each $g \in G$. For $H \le K \le G$ put $xH \mathrel{\theta_K} yH \iff x^{-1}y \in K$; its classes are the sets $\lbrace xkH : k \in K \rbrace$, each with $[K:H]$ elements. Conversely a $G$-invariant equivalence $\theta$ (a block system) gives the subgroup $K = \lbrace g : gH \mathrel{\theta} H \rbrace$. The two maps are inverse and preserve order, so $\operatorname{Con}(G/H) \cong [H, G]$, and an interval isomorphic to a lattice $L$ gives a finite algebra, on $[G:H]$ elements, whose congruence lattice is $L$. For example, $G = S_3$ and $H = 1$ give the lattice $M_4$, because the four subgroups between $1$ and $S_3$ (three of order 2, and $A_3$) pairwise meet in $1$ and generate $S_3$; with $H = \langle (1\,2) \rangle$, which is maximal, the interval has two elements and $S_3$ acting on three points preserves only the two trivial partitions.

W. DeMeo (arXiv:1204.4305, 2012) proved that every lattice with at most seven elements is representable with one possible exception, called L10 in the DeMeo–Freese–Jipsen manuscript and L7 in his thesis. It is the product of a two-element and a three-element chain with one more element that is a complement of every other element except the top and bottom. On 28 August 2026 Chenxiao Tian posted a seven-page note, "The exceptional seven-element lattice as a subgroup interval in PSL2(64)", representing L10 as $[S_3, \mathrm{PSL}(2,64)]$ with $S_3 = \mathrm{SL}(2,2)$ embedded through the subfield $\mathrm{GF}(2)$; the five intermediate subgroups are $D_{18}$, $D_{42}$, $A_5$, $D_{126}$ and $\mathrm{PSL}(2,8)$. The algebra has $262{,}080 / 6 = 43{,}680$ elements.

The note is public: the report `docs/L10-is-representable.md` in UniversalAlgebra/fin-lat-rep-gap cites it and links a copy on ResearchGate. ResearchGate refused access (HTTP 403) when this survey was checked, so the note itself has not been read here. The same report, dated 17 September 2026, came in with pull request #2, which williamdemeo opened on 17 September and merged on 25 September. It recomputes Tian's interval in GAP from his two generating matrices with `IntermediateSubgroups`, gets the five subgroups and the nine covering pairs he states, and confirms each lemma of his proof separately; the isomorphism test of the agda-algebras project agrees. A scan of the 414 tables of marks in GAP's TomLib then finds six upper intervals isomorphic to L10, in $\mathrm{PSL}(2,64)$, $\mathrm{Sp}(6,2)$, $2.\mathrm{Sp}(6,2)$, McL, McL.2 and Co3, all recomputed explicitly. The smallest algebra comes from $\mathrm{Sp}(6,2)$, of order $2^9 \cdot 3 \cdot 15 \cdot 63 = 1{,}451{,}520$, over a core-free Frobenius group $7{:}6$ of order 42 and index 34,560; the intermediate subgroups are $S_7$, $L_3(2){:}2$, $\mathrm{P\Gamma L}(2,8)$, $S_8$ and $U_3(3){:}2$. So the smallest algebra representing L10 has between 16 and 34,560 elements. The repository's README now says that L10 "is the congruence lattice of a finite algebra after all". The report says it was written with AI assistance (Claude Fable 5.1 and Claude Opus 5); the outputs of every run are committed beside the programs.

What the case needed was a written proof and an independent check. Tian's note is the written proof, and DeMeo's recomputation, by someone other than Tian, is the check; the $\mathrm{Sp}(6,2)$ interval is a second witness found by other means. What remains is refereeing, and optionally a machine-checked certificate, which the report sizes at 847 double cosets and about a fifth of a megabyte for $\mathrm{Sp}(6,2)$. The manuscript repository UniversalAlgebra/fin-lat-rep, where the article itself lives, is not publicly readable (it returned 404 when checked). The next case, a lattice with eight elements, is still unnamed: none of these sources lists which of them are unrepresented.

For anyone who wants to rerun the check, three details from the GAP source matter. At index 200 or more, `IntermediateSubgroups(G, H)` works down through maximal subgroups; its method for small index computes `AllBlocks` of the action on the cosets instead. So recomputing the interval as the block systems of that action really is a second code path. `FactorCosetAction(G, H)` returns a homomorphism, so `AllBlocks` has to be applied to its image. And `AllBlocks` returns one block from each block system, the one containing the smallest moved point.

### Hadamard conjecture: order 668

[Linear algebra, matrices and representation theory](./linear-algebra-and-representation-theory.md). A Hadamard matrix of order $n$ is an $n \times n$ matrix of $\pm1$ entries with $HH^T = nI$, and the conjecture says one exists for every multiple of 4. In 2014 Đoković, Golubitsky and Kotsireas listed twelve open orders below 2000, the smallest 668. On 12 August 2026 Levent Alpöge, Philippe Voinov and Saul Reynolds-Haertle, working with Anthropic's model Claude, announced matrices for all twelve on X.

The matrices are public. The announcement and Alpöge's reply to it carry them, and OEIS A007299 links a Pastebin copy of the order-668 matrix and a folder with the others. Ion Nechita wrote on 13 August that he checked $HH^T = nI$ with a short Python program; vibemathed.com reports reproducing all twelve in exact integer arithmetic on 12 August. Wikipedia's article now records the construction, and Epoch AI marks the problem solved, calling its attribution to AI provisional. There is no refereed paper, and Kulhandjian's preprint of 22 September (arXiv:2609.25543) still calls 668 "the smallest open Hadamard order". This survey did not download the matrices or run the check itself.

After the twelve orders comes a real search. John D. Cook's post of 13 August 2026 says order 2004 is not known. But 2003 is prime and $2003 \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, so Paley's 1933 construction, which gives order $q + 1$ for every prime power $q \equiv 3 \pmod 4$, already produces a Hadamard matrix of order 2004. One of the judges noticed this, and the primality of 2003 was checked by computer; the linear algebra file now says so too. The true next open order has to come from the Đoković–Golubitsky–Kotsireas tables.

## Runners-up still open

### Kuzmin's conjecture, $n = 5$

[Rings, modules and algebras](./rings-and-algebras.md). If every element of an associative algebra over a field of characteristic 0 satisfies $x^n = 0$, then all products of some fixed length vanish, and $d(n)$ is the least such length. Kuzmin conjectured in 1975 that $d(n) = n(n+1)/2$. It is known for $n \le 4$, the last case by Vaughan-Lee's 1993 computer proof. The open case is one number: is $d(5) = 15$? What is known is $15 \le d(5) \le 25$, the upper bound from Razmyslov's $d(n) \le n^2$.

Here too either answer would be a new theorem, and unlike the Lieb case the question is finite linear algebra. It is also by far the heaviest. The question lives among the multilinear polynomials of degree 15, a space of dimension $15! \approx 1.3 \times 10^{12}$. Starting from Romano's 2022 reformulation in terms of $S_m$-modules, the proof judge splits it into one rank computation for each of the 176 partitions of 15, and the largest piece has dimension 292,864, for the partition $(5,4,3,2,1)$. That figure is the proof judge's; the hook length formula over all 176 partitions of 15 confirms it. Rank computations modulo a prime on matrices of that size need a computing cluster and careful engineering. Only the proof judge ranked it, third, and the survey gives no reason why nobody has run it.

### The 1/3–2/3 conjecture, posets on 15 elements

[Homological algebra, category theory, K-theory, universal algebra and lattices](./homological-categorical-and-universal-algebra.md). Every finite poset that is not a chain should have a pair $x, y$ for which the probability that $x$ comes before $y$, in a linear extension chosen uniformly at random, lies between $1/3$ and $2/3$. Gupta's preprint (arXiv:2607.23926, July 2026) checks all 1,338,193,159,771 unlabeled posets on 14 elements, so 15 elements is next. The computation judge mentioned it only in passing. There are 68,275,077,901,156 posets on 15 elements (OEIS A000112), about 51 times as many. The search is exhaustive, its likely result is "no counterexample", and that result can be checked only by rerunning a computation of the same size. The Lieb case is the opposite: if it is solved, the certificate is small enough for anyone to check.
